How to Write AI Art Prompts in Different Styles

P

PromptNest Team

Published August 2026

The same subject can look wildly different depending on the style words you choose, and honestly, this took me a while to fully appreciate. A "fox in a forest" can turn into a soft storybook illustration or a razor-sharp nature photograph, and the model isn't randomly deciding which, it's entirely a function of how you described the style. Once I started deliberately swapping style vocabulary on the same subject just to see what happened, the whole thing clicked. Here's how to get three of the most popular styles right.

Anime style

Anime prompts respond well to specific visual vocabulary rather than just the word "anime" sitting on its own with nothing to back it up. Try combining it with cues like cel-shaded, vibrant colors, expressive eyes, or a named studio-inspired look like Studio Ghibli-inspired, which gives the model a much more concrete target than the genre name alone.

Example

"A young adventurer standing on a cliff overlooking a valley, anime style, cel-shaded, Studio Ghibli-inspired, vibrant colors, dramatic sky."

Photorealistic style

Photorealistic prompts benefit enormously from camera and lighting language, the same terms an actual photographer would reach for. Words like shot on DSLR, 85mm lens, shallow depth of field, and natural lighting push the model toward convincing realism instead of drifting into that slightly-illustrated look that happens when you just say "realistic" and leave it there.

Example

"A young adventurer standing on a cliff overlooking a valley, photorealistic, shot on DSLR, 85mm lens, golden hour lighting, shallow depth of field."

Watercolor style

Watercolor prompts lean into softness and texture, think soft edges, bleeding colors, and visible paper texture rather than crisp digital lines. Naming the medium explicitly, watercolor painting rather than just "painting," keeps the model from defaulting to a generic digital-art interpretation that doesn't actually capture what makes watercolor look like watercolor.

Example

"A young adventurer standing on a cliff overlooking a valley, watercolor painting, soft edges, bleeding colors, visible paper texture, muted palette."

Same subject, same composition, completely different feeling.

The pattern to remember

Notice the subject stayed identical across all three examples above, only the style-specific vocabulary changed each time. That's the fastest way to explore different looks without starting over, lock in your subject and composition first, then swap out just the style phrase to see the full range of what's possible from a single idea.
