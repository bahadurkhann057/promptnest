10 Tips for Writing Better Midjourney Prompts

P

PromptNest Team

Published August 2026

I've generated a genuinely embarrassing number of Midjourney images at this point, most of them mediocre, some of them exactly what I wanted, a few of them better than what I could've described even if I'd tried harder. Here's what actually moved the needle, not the generic advice you see copy-pasted everywhere.

1. Lead with the subject, not the mood

It's tempting to open with atmosphere, "a moody, cinematic..." but Midjourney weighs earlier words more heavily. Start with what the image is actually of, then layer the mood and style on after.

2. Vague style words waste your prompt

"Digital art" tells the model almost nothing, there are a million things that could mean. Naming something specific, like Ukiyo-e woodblock print or 1980s airbrush illustration, gives it an actual anchor to work from.

Try this instead of "digital art"

"a fox in a forest, ukiyo-e woodblock print style, muted indigo and rust palette"

3. Lighting does more than people expect

I underestimated this for way too long. Two identical prompts with just the lighting phrase swapped, golden hour lighting versus dramatic chiaroscuro, will come back looking like they're from different projects entirely.

4. Say where the camera is

Low-angle shot, wide establishing shot, close-up framing, these push the model toward an actual composition instead of defaulting to whatever centered pose it usually reaches for.

5. Commas beat sentences

This one felt counterintuitive at first, but Midjourney parses a list of descriptive phrases better than a proper sentence. It's reading for concepts, not grammar.

Write it like a list of ingredients, not a story.

6. Add something for quality, but don't overdo it

Highly detailed, sharp focus, that kind of phrase nudges toward more refined output. One or two is enough, stacking five of them doesn't multiply the effect, it just eats your prompt's attention.

7. Mood words matter even in non-people scenes

Eerie, serene, triumphant, these shift color grading and atmosphere even in a landscape with nothing emotional happening in it. Worth testing on a scene you think doesn't need it.

8. Don't fight yourself with contradictions

"Minimalist" and "highly detailed" in the same prompt just confuses things. Pick one, generate, then try the other version separately if you want to compare.

9. Treat the first result as a draft, not a verdict

A weak first output usually isn't the model failing, it's information. Change one or two things instead of starting from scratch, and you'll get there faster than you'd expect.

10. Keep the combinations that work

When a style, lighting, and composition combo clicks, write it down somewhere. It usually transfers well to a completely different subject too, which saves a lot of reinventing.
