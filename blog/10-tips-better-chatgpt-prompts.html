10 Tips for Writing Better ChatGPT Prompts

Published August 2026

ChatGPT and similar chat assistants respond to structure, not just wording. A short, vague request forces the model to guess what you actually want. These tips help you guess less and get more of what you need on the first try.

1. Give it a role

Starting with "Act as a..." or "You are a..." primes the model to answer with the tone, vocabulary, and priorities of that role — a marketing consultant answers differently than a strict editor.

2. State the task clearly, separate from context

Put background information first, then clearly state what you actually want done. Mixing them together makes it easy for the model to miss the actual ask.

3. Specify the format you want back

If you want a table, a numbered list, or a short paragraph, say so directly. Without a format instruction, the model defaults to whatever seems generically appropriate.

4. Set a length

"Keep it under 100 words" or "write a detailed, in-depth answer" changes the output dramatically. Don't leave length to guesswork if it matters to you.

5. Give an example when the task is unusual

For tasks that are hard to describe in words alone — a specific tone, a particular format — showing one example output is often more effective than a longer explanation.

6. Ask for reasoning on complex questions

Adding "explain your reasoning" or "think through this step by step" tends to produce more accurate, more carefully considered answers on multi-step problems.

7. Break big tasks into smaller prompts

Instead of one giant prompt asking for an entire report, ask for an outline first, review it, then ask for each section. You'll get more control and better quality per section.

8. Tell it what to avoid, not just what to include

"Don't use jargon" or "avoid generic advice" narrows the response in ways that positive instructions alone often miss.

9. Iterate rather than starting over

If a response is close but not quite right, ask for a specific revision ("make the tone more casual") rather than rewriting your entire original prompt.

10. Tell it who the audience is

"Explain this to a complete beginner" versus "explain this to a senior engineer" changes vocabulary, depth, and assumed background dramatically.
